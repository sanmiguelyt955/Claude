Create 3D Game Models for Free: Opus 5.5 + Blender + Tripo (15 Steps)
The people telling you 3D is hard are the same people selling you the course. I made it free. 
You can make game-ready 3D models in Blender, which is free, with Opus writing the code. Props cost nothing per model: Opus writes Python and Blender builds them. Characters cost nothing on your own GPU, or about $0.20 to $0.60 per model on Tripo. Everything after the mesh (cleanup, rig, weights, clips, export) runs free in Blender.
This guide also answers the two questions I get most: why low-poly props from an AI sheet come out as floating boxes, and why clothes stretch when a Tripo character moves.
The short version:
Free: props, buildings and vehicles in Python in Blender (steps 02 to 05)
Free on your own card, or under $1 on Tripo: characters and creatures (steps 06 to 08)
Free for every model: rig, weights, clips and export in Blender (steps 09 to 15)
Set up once:
Install Blender (free) and Claude Code. Run every prompt below in Claude Code in the terminal, in one project folder. It writes the Python, runs Blender with no window and opens its own renders.
Tell Claude where Blender is. On a Mac it's /Applications/Blender.app/Contents/MacOS/Blender. Put that path in CLAUDE.md with this line: "Run Blender as: blender -b file.blend --python script.py".
Keep every step as a .py file, so you can rerun it after any change.
Words you'll see:
Mesh: the 3D shape, made of triangles. GLB and FBX: the files it's saved in.
Orthographic view: a flat view with no perspective, like a blueprint.
UV and texture: the picture wrapped on the mesh. Normal map: a texture that fakes small detail.
Decimate: cut the triangle count.
Armature (rig): the skeleton. Weights: how much each bone moves each vertex.
Rest pose: the pose the skeleton is built in. A-pose: arms down at about 45 degrees.
IK: the foot or hand stays put and the leg or arm bends to reach it.

Paid route, minimum price:

Pay only for the generation. Tripo also sells retopology (10 to 40 credits) and auto-rig (25 credits); steps 08 to 12 do both in Blender for free.
PART 2 · LOW-POLY PROPS IN PURE CODE
02. Make a reference you can measure
A typical AI sheet shows the prop in perspective, with shadows, and the views disagree: a bipod folded in the side view and open in the 3D view. Claude can look at that image, but it can't measure it, so it guesses coordinates and the parts float.
Ask ChatGPT, or any image generator, for views made for measuring:
Ask for flat orthographic views, because a perspective sheet can't be measured and every guessed number becomes a gap.
If you have only one image with several views on it, ask Claude to cut it into separate views first. If a view is missing, give that size by hand (for example, the stock is 5 cm wide).

For buildings and whole streets, ask for a generator instead of single models:
Don't send buildings and street props to an image-to-3D generator. It returns almost square footprints, so a bus or a fence stretched to its real length goes flat, and the textures come out noisy instead of clean.
PART 3 · CHARACTERS FROM A GENERATOR
06. Draw the sheet for the generator
Draw the character with empty hands, because anything in a hand melts into it and you can't cut it out again. Make the props on their own sheet.
Image tools often draw the left view as a mirror of the right, so a strap or weapon jumps sides. Send only true views.
07. Put every view in its slot
Ask Claude to cut the sheet into view_front.png, view_right.png and view_back.png, one view per file, same height.
Tripo needs an API key from its developer platform and some credits. Put the key in an environment variable and ask Claude to write a small client from Tripo's API docs: upload the images, start a multiview task, poll it, download the GLB.
Tripo's multiview API takes up to 4 images as front, left, back and right. Front is required, 2 views are the minimum.
Put each view in the slot of the side it shows, because a side view in the wrong slot can give the character a second face on the back of the head.
Local GPU instead of Tripo (Windows with an NVIDIA card): run Hunyuan3D-2-WinPortable (search GitHub for that name), which starts a local server on port 8080. Ask Claude to send the same views to that server in multiview mode with texture on and save the GLB. On an 8 GB laptop card that took about 4 minutes per model.
To spend less: check the views before you send them (a wrong view is a paid redo), and skip texture for background characters you'll paint flat in the engine.

08. Clean the mesh before any bone
Budget: up to 20,000 triangles for Roblox (its limit per mesh), 40,000 to 70,000 for a Unity hero, 3,000 for crowds. Tripo GLBs come in facing +X.
Merge vertices before weighting (line 2), because duplicated seam vertices get different weights and tear open in motion.
Make the 3,000-triangle crowd copy too: a crowd of full 40,000-triangle models drops the frame rate hard.
PART 4 · RIGGING WITHOUT STRETCHED CLOTHES
A generator hands you one fused shell: the coat is welded to the legs, the sleeves to the hands. Automatic weights can't tell clothes from body, so a raised arm pulls the coat and the chest with it. Steps 09 to 12 fix that.
09. Split the shell into parts
Tag by position as well as colour, because a dark coat patch tagged as scarf follows the scarf and leaves a hole in the back.
10. Read the joints off a grid
Check the dots before any bone: a shoulder joint 3 cm too high stretches the whole sleeve. Use Mixamo bone names (Hips, Spine, LeftArm...) and Unity's Humanoid importer maps the rig by itself. Animals take the same steps with their own joints (spine, tail, four legs) and import as Generic.
11. Set weights by rules
If automatic weights fail on a generated mesh with holes, bind a voxel-remeshed copy of it instead, then transfer the weights back to the real mesh.
Never smooth weights across two tagged parts, because smoothing leaks the hand bone into the coat and pulls a spike out of it. Smooth only around shoulders, elbows and knees, inside one part.
Use the rules when the clothes follow the arms. They also made the head turn and the step worse on this model: the collar, weighted to the chest, touches the head, and the hip weight runs down the thighs. Run step 12 and fix those seams vertex by vertex.
12. Test poses and measure the tears
Keep shoulder turns near the rest pose, because a generated mesh has no spare geometry in the armpit and big turns tear the upper arm.
PART 5 · CLIPS AND THE ENGINE
13. Write clips on one rest pose
For a combat clip, the same pattern:
Write clips on the armature they will play on, because a clip copied onto a rig with a different rest pose breaks the legs.
14. Check every clip by numbers
A pose that looks wrong still needs your eyes.
15. Export for your engine
Leave Apply Transform off for rigged meshes, because Blender's own docs mark it as broken with armatures. Step 08 already applied the transforms.
In Unity:
Humanoid for two arms, two legs and a head; Generic for animals, with a root bone you name.
Set Loop Time on import; the FBX doesn't carry it.
In Roblox:
File, then Import 3D. Rig type Custom for your own skeleton.
Keep Zero Influence Bones on if you have helper bones, like weapon sockets. It's off by default.
Bones need scale 1 and rotation 0, the root at 0,0,0 and no skin weight on Root. Max 4 bones per vertex.
1024 px textures are the safe size; normal maps are OpenGL.
Put a small probe cube in the file and measure it after import: the importer can change the scale of the whole file.
When it breaks
Prop parts float: the sheet was in perspective. Steps 02 and 03.
Session limit hit on one prop: one part per prompt, 768 px renders. Step 04.
Second face on the back of the head: a view in the wrong slot. Step 07.
Extra tail or limb: the generator saw only the front. Send the back view.
Prop melted into the hand: empty hands on the sheet. Step 06.
Clothes stretch with the arms: torso and coat weighted to arm bones. Step 11.
Spike out of the coat: weights smoothed across parts. Step 11.
Head or hips tear when turning: a rigid part meets another bone's weights at the seam. Step 12.
Twisted knees in a clip: the clip came from a different rest pose. Step 13.
Boots point backwards: turn the foot 180 degrees about the shin before rigging.
White discs or black triangles: faces with a zero-length normal. Step 08.
Thin wires, text and logos missing: generators drop them. Build those parts in code.
